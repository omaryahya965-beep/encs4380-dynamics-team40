Example: a thermometer dipped into tea

A thermometer with τ = 10 s moves from room air at 20 °C into tea at 80 °C. That is a 60 °C step with K = 1:

T(t) = 20 + 60(1 − e−t/10) °C

  1. t = 0 s20.0 °Cstill at room temperature
  2. t = 10 s = τ57.9 °C20 + 0.632 × 60, 63.2% of the step
  3. t = 30 s = 3τ77.0 °C20 + 0.950 × 60, 95% of the step

Why only about 58 °C after 10 s? After one time constant, any first-order sensor has covered 1 − e−1 = 63.2% of a step, and 0.632 × 60 °C ≈ 38 °C above the starting 20 °C. Read it then and you are about 22 °C low, even though the thermometer is perfectly calibrated: it simply has not caught up yet.

    Why 63.2% and 95%?

    Put t = τ into y(t) = KA(1 − e−t/τ) and you get KA(1 − e−1) = 0.632 KA. At t = 3τ, 1 − e−3 = 0.950. These fractions are the same for every K and τ, so you can read τ straight off a measured step response: it is the moment the output reaches 63.2% of its final value.

    Why the ramp error never disappears

    The sensor output only moves when there is a gap: τ dy/dt = Kx − y. To keep climbing at the ramp’s rate, dy/dt = KA, the gap must settle at Kx − y = KAτ. The output becomes a copy of the ideal ramp shifted right by τ: y(t) → KA(t − τ).

    With K = 1.0 and τ = 2.0 s, the ideal output climbs 1.00 units every second and the sensor is 2.00 s behind, so it sits 1.00 × 2.00 = 2.00 units below the ideal line.

      Understanding the parameters

      What is a first-order sensor?

      A sensor whose dynamics are dominated by one energy-storage element: the heat stored in a thermometer bulb, or the charge on an RC filter’s capacitor. Its output cannot jump. It moves toward K·x at a rate proportional to the remaining gap: τ dy/dt = Kx − y.

      What does τ mean physically?

      τ compares how much energy the sensor must store with how easily it exchanges energy. For a thermometer, τ = mc/(hA): heat capacity divided by heat-transfer coefficient times surface area. A bigger bulb, or still air instead of stirred water, means a larger τ.

      What happens when τ increases?

      Every response time stretches in proportion: the 63.2% point (τ), the 95% point (3τ) and the 10–90% rise time (2.2τ). Doubling τ doubles all of them. The final value KA does not change.

      What does the gain K change?

      K is the static sensitivity, in output units per input unit (for example, mV per °C). It scales the final value KA and the whole curve vertically, but not the timing: the output still reaches 63.2% of its final value at t = τ.

      Why is there a steady-state error for a ramp?

      To keep climbing at the ramp’s rate KA, the output needs a constant gap Kx − y = KAτ to drive it. So it runs parallel to the ideal ramp, τ seconds late. The same delay shows up as an error of KAτ in output units, or as a lag of τ in seconds.

      Why is the logarithmic error plot useful?

      On a log axis, f(t) = e−t/τ becomes a straight line whose slope gives τ. Fitting that line to measured samples uses all of the data rather than one 63.2% reading, so the estimate is robust to noise, and a curved plot warns that the sensor is not truly first order.

      Why does a sensor ring before it settles?

      Springs, accelerometers and pressure gauges store energy in two ways, so they can overshoot and oscillate instead of creeping up to the answer. Change the damping and the natural frequency, and see how fast, how far and for how long the output swings.

                Understanding the parameters

                What makes a sensor second order?

                Two independent energy stores that trade energy back and forth: a mass and a spring, or an inductor and a capacitor. That exchange is what lets the output swing past its final value, which a first-order sensor can never do.

                What does ζ control?

                How quickly the oscillation is drained. Low ζ means a long, ringing response, ζ = 1 is the edge of oscillation, and high ζ adds friction that mostly just slows the sensor down. Overshoot depends on ζ alone.

                What does ωn control?

                The speed of the whole response. Double ωn and every time on the curve (tr, tp, ts) halves, while the shape, and so the overshoot percentage, stays exactly the same.

                Why is overshoot only a function of ζ?

                Changing ωn just rescales time. The curve is stretched horizontally, so the ratio of the peak to the final value cannot change. Only ζ changes the curve’s actual shape.

                What do the poles tell you?

                The real part sets how fast the response dies out, the imaginary part how fast it rings. Poles farther left settle faster, poles farther from the real axis oscillate faster, and ζ is the cosine of the angle they make with the negative real axis.

                Why do designers like ζ near 0.7?

                It gives about 4.6% overshoot with a rise time close to the best possible without a large ring. Critical damping is safe but slower to rise, and heavier damping only adds delay.

                How to estimate the parameters

                First-order step: Find the final output KA. For a unit step, K is the final output. The time at which the response first reaches 63.2% of the final change is approximately τ.

                First-order sine: Use the input and output phase shift to estimate τ = tan(|φ|)/ω; then K = output amplitude × √(1 + (ωτ)²) for a unit-amplitude sine.

                Second-order step: Use overshoot OS to estimate ζ, then peak time tp to estimate ωn. For the underdamped case: ζ = −ln(OS) / √(π² + ln²(OS)); ωn = π / (tp√(1 − ζ²)). Every second-order sensor in this challenge is underdamped, so the curve always has a peak to read.
